What a customer is worth

Every budget conversation ends at the same question: how much can we afford to pay for one more customer? Answer it with a feeling and the budget is set by whoever argues loudest. Answer it with three numbers, what a customer cost, how fast they pay it back, and what they are worth over their life, and the argument moves to the assumptions, which is where it belongs. Tonight we work all three for one Cadence Teams account on one sheet, and finish with a sentence you can put in a plan.

Where we start

Part 1 · covers CAC

What one customer cost to win

5 min live

Customer acquisition cost, CAC, is the simplest number in marketing and the one most often quoted wrong. The arithmetic never changes: what you spent to win customers in a period, divided by the customers you won in that period. What changes is what you count as "spent", and the cheap version of the number usually leaves out most of the money.

LiveOne formula, four versions, and which one to use5 min▶

CAC = sales and marketing spend in a period ÷ new customers won in that period. Two choices sit inside that line, and every disagreement about CAC is one of them.

  1. Fully loaded or paid media only. Fully loaded counts everything you spent to win customers: media, content and events, the salaries of the people who sell and market, their tools, the agency. Paid media only counts what the ad platforms billed. For Cadence (constructed) that is $1,800 against $500 for the same new account.
  2. Blended or per channel. Blended divides all spend by all new customers, including the ones who arrived on their own. Per channel divides one channel's spend by the customers that channel won, which depends on how you credit a customer who touched three channels: that is the attribution question, owned by learn-marketing-attribution-with-phoebe.

Which one for which decision. To decide whether the business can afford its growth, use fully loaded and blended: it is the number the bank account feels. To decide where the next dollar goes, use per channel, and use the marginal version where you can, because the last customer a channel won cost more than its average. Paid media only is a useful number for the person running the ads and a dangerous one in a board deck. Whichever you pick, write the definition next to the number; a CAC with no definition is two people agreeing on a word and disagreeing on the money.

One timing trap. A Teams deal that takes two months to close was paid for by last quarter's spend and counted in this quarter's customers. Long cycles make a single month's CAC jump around; a rolling three-month figure is steadier.

Self-studyThe session 4 bench as the worked example4 min▶

The funnel bench in session 4 is a CAC calculator with the definition fixed: one $20,000 monthly budget, constructed cost per lead by channel, and close rates measured on the real 2018 Olist cohort (5,998 leads, 781 closed deals). At the printed defaults, the same $20,000 buys a customer for $368 under the best split and $627 under "cheapest leads first". Nothing about the budget changed. Only where it went.

That is the second lesson of CAC: it is not a property of your product, it is a property of your spending. The spend is constructed on the bench; the close rates are real. So the bench tells you the shape of the trap, cheap leads that rarely close, and not the dollar value your company would see.

The bench also shows the definition problem in miniature. It counts only media spend at a cost per lead, so it is a paid-media-only CAC. Load it with salaries and tools and every preset rises, and the ranking survives.

Real world

Same budget, a 70% gap. On the session 4 bench, with close rates measured on real Olist leads and costs constructed, $20,000 a month bought customers at $368 each when it followed the deals and at $627 each when it followed the cheapest leads. A team that reports "our CAC" without saying how the budget was split is reporting a number that could move by that much next month with no change to the product, the price or the team.

Part 2 · covers payback

Months until the customer has paid for themselves

5 min live

CAC alone cannot tell you whether $1,800 is a lot. Payback can: payback months = CAC ÷ monthly gross profit per customer. It is the number of months a customer must stay before the money spent to win them has come back. David Skok, in "SaaS Metrics 2.0", gives the reference points: "Many of the best SaaS businesses are able to recover their CAC in 5-7 months." And he writes that profitability "is anemic if the time to recover CAC extends beyond 12 months". Read that second line carefully: 12 months is his ceiling, where things start to look weak, not a target to aim at.

LiveWhy gross profit, not revenue, and why Cadence takes 20 months5 min▶

A Cadence Teams account pays $120 a month. It does not hand the company $120 a month to spend: hosting the recordings, transcription, payment fees and support come out first. With a 75% gross margin (constructed), what is left to pay back the acquisition cost is $120 × 0.75 = $90. Divide by revenue instead and payback looks like 15 months, which is a cheerful answer to a question nobody asked. The money that repays CAC is the money left after delivering the product.

So Cadence's payback is $1,800 ÷ $90 = 20 months. Measured against Skok's own reference points, that is well past his 12-month ceiling and nowhere near his 5-7 month band. It does not mean Cadence is doomed. It means every Teams account must stay at least 20 months before it has made the company a dollar, and that 1 in 40 accounts leaving each month (2.5%) is going to keep a lot of them from getting there.

Payback has one big virtue over the other metrics tonight: it needs no forecast. CAC is measured, gross profit per month is measured, and the answer is a count of months you can check against real accounts.

Self-studyPayback is a cash question3 min▶

The CAC is paid now; the gross profit arrives over 20 months. Someone funds the gap. Win 50 Teams accounts this month at $1,800 each and $90,000 leaves the bank today, while those accounts return $4,500 a month between them. Growth with a long payback is a business that gets poorer the faster it succeeds, until the early cohorts start paying back. That is why finance teams ask about payback before they ask about lifetime value: payback is the shape of the cash hole.

Gross margin and cash timing are finance's subject, and learn-finance-with-phoebe teaches both, along with the three statements where the hole shows up. It does not teach CAC or LTV; those stay here. If your gross margin is a guess, fix that first: every number below inherits it.

An annual prepaid plan changes the cash shape without changing the payback arithmetic. If the customer pays twelve months up front, the hole is smaller even though the profit per month is the same.

Real world

The bench crossed Skok's ceiling on its own. The session 4 bench prints payback next to CAC, using a constructed $52 of monthly gross profit per new seller. The best split pays back in 7.1 months; "cheapest leads first" takes 12.1 months, just past the 12 months at which Skok calls profitability anemic. Same budget, same product, same assumed gross profit: one choice of where to spend moved the business from near his best band to past his ceiling.

Part 3 · covers LTV and the ratio

Lifetime value is a forecast with a formula on top

5 min live

Lifetime value, LTV, asks how much gross profit a customer brings over the whole time they stay. Divide it by CAC and you get the ratio budgets are argued over. Skok's reference point, from the same article: "The best SaaS businesses have a LTV to CAC ratio that is higher than 3, sometimes as high as 7 or 8." The retention course on the shelf calls 3:1 the classic healthy benchmark and credits Skok, which is consistent: 3 is the floor of the good range, not its middle. The catch is that LTV is not measured. It is a forecast, and the formula hides the forecast inside one input.

LiveThe formula, and the four things it assumes5 min▶

The common simple form, the one Skok uses: LTV = monthly gross profit per customer ÷ monthly churn rate, which is the same as average revenue per account × gross margin ÷ churn. For Cadence: $90 ÷ 0.025 = $3,600. Against a $1,800 CAC that is a ratio of 2.0, below Skok's 3.

Dividing by churn is a compact way of saying "multiply by the average lifetime". At 2.5% leaving each month the average Teams account stays 1 ÷ 0.025 = 40 months. That one number carries the whole forecast. Four assumptions sit inside it:

  1. Churn is constant. Real cohorts usually lose more customers early and fewer later. A single rate averages over that shape.
  2. No expansion. The formula assumes the 8 seats stay 8 seats. Teams that add seats grow the value; teams that shrink cut it.
  3. No discounting. A dollar of gross profit in month 38 counts the same as one this month.
  4. The churn rate came from somewhere. On a young product it was measured on a young cohort, often over fewer months than the 40 it is now predicting.

Practitioners disagree about the second and third. Some add expansion revenue and report a larger LTV; others leave it out on purpose, because counting on growth you have not yet seen flatters the number used to justify spending. Some discount future profit at a cost of capital; others argue the discount rate is one more assumption and keep the simple form. There is no settled answer. The professional norm is to say which version you used, every time you quote the number.

Self-studyThree levers, three owners4 min▶

The ratio has three inputs that matter and each belongs to a different team. All numbers below are constructed for Cadence, one lever moved at a time with the others held where they are.

  • Price and seats (monthly gross profit). 10 seats instead of 8: $150 a month, $112.50 of gross profit, payback 16.0 months, LTV $4,500, ratio 2.5. Pricing and how demand responds to it are taught in learn-economics-with-phoebe.
  • Churn (the lifetime). 1.5% instead of 2.5%: LTV $6,000, ratio 3.33. Payback is still 20 months, because payback does not look at churn at all. What moves churn, and how retention reshapes LTV, is learn-customer-retention-with-phoebe.
  • CAC (the cost). $1,200 instead of $1,800: payback 13.3 months, ratio 3.0. It reaches 3.0 and stops there. This is the lever session 4 put on the bench.

Notice what the figure shows: the lever marketing controls most directly, CAC, is the one that only just reaches the line. The biggest move came from churn, which marketing influences only through who it brings in. That is the strongest argument for session 1's not-for list: the cheapest way to raise LTV is to stop acquiring customers who will leave.

Real world

Olist could not have computed this. Of the 842 sellers who closed in the public Olist funnel, 797 declared a monthly revenue of 0, and the median of all closed deals is 0. Any LTV built on that column would be a forecast with nothing underneath it. The session 4 bench had to state one revenue assumption instead and say so. Before you trust an LTV, find the column it was computed from and check whether anyone filled it in.

Build-along

The Cadence account on one sheet

★ 22 min · everyone builds

A spreadsheet or a calculator and a sheet of paper. Inputs on the left, outputs on the right, and every output computed from the inputs, never typed. Every Cadence number is constructed; once the sheet works, replace them with yours and keep the formulas.

Type the six inputs, and nothing else. Put each in its own cell with its unit in the label. MRR is the one input you can compute (price × seats); compute it, so that changing seats later flows through.

★ Step 1 · inputs (constructed)Price per seat, a month $15 Seats per account 8 MRR per account $120 (= 15 x 8) Gross margin 75% Monthly logo churn 2.5% Blended CAC, fully loaded $1,800

Monthly gross profit. Revenue times margin. This is the number every other output divides by or multiplies, so check it twice.

★ Step 2 · monthly gross profitGross profit per account = MRR x gross margin = 120 x 0.75 = $90 a month

Payback. CAC over monthly gross profit. Write Skok's two reference points next to it so nobody reads 20 as normal.

★ Step 3 · payback monthsPayback = CAC / monthly gross profit = 1,800 / 90 = 20 months Skok: best SaaS recover CAC in 5-7 months; profitability anemic beyond 12 months (a ceiling, not a target)

LTV and the ratio. Add a cell for the implied average lifetime too (1 ÷ churn), because that is the forecast you are trusting.

★ Step 4 · LTV and LTV:CACImplied lifetime = 1 / 0.025 = 40 months LTV = 90 / 0.025 = $3,600 LTV : CAC = 3,600 / 1,800 = 2.0 (Skok's best: higher than 3) Assumes: constant churn, no expansion, no discounting, churn measured on whatever cohort you had

Three sensitivities, one at a time. Change one input, read all four outputs, put it back, then change the next. Moving two at once tells you nothing about either.

★ Step 5 · one lever at a time (constructed)Churn 2.5% -> 1.5% LTV $6,000 ratio 3.33 payback 20 months (unchanged) Seats 8 -> 10 MRR $150 gross profit $112.50 payback 16.0 months LTV $4,500 ratio 2.5 CAC $1,800 -> $1,200 payback 13.3 months LTV $3,600 ratio 3.0 Crosses 3 first: churn (3.33). CAC reaches 3.0 exactly.

Write the one sentence for the plan, and choose a rule. Two common rules give two different ceilings. A 12-month payback rule says Cadence can pay at most $1,080; a ratio-of-3 rule says $1,200. They disagree because one is about cash and the other is about lifetime value. Pick one, write why, and put the sentence in session 6's plan.

★ Step 6 · the plan sentenceRule A, payback of 12 months: max CAC = 12 x 90 = $1,080 Rule B, LTV:CAC of 3: max CAC = 3,600 / 3 = $1,200 The two rules disagree by $120. Choose one: "We can pay up to $1,080 to win a Teams account, because we fund acquisition from cash and will not wait more than 12 months to get it back. Today's blended CAC is $1,800, so the gap is $720 per account."

Keep the sheet. Session 6 assembles the one-page plan, and the ceiling you chose tonight is its budget line. If the gap between today's CAC and the ceiling is large, as it is for Cadence, the plan has to say which lever closes it and who owns that lever.

Homework

Before session 6

30 min
  1. Compute your own product's payback with your real gross margin, not revenue. If you do not know the margin, ask finance for it; that conversation is the homework.
  2. Write your CAC definition in one line (fully loaded or paid media only, blended or per channel, which months) and check whether the CAC in your last report used the same one.
  3. Find where your churn rate came from. How many months of data, which cohort, how many customers. Then compute the implied lifetime (1 ÷ churn) and compare it with how long your product has existed.
  4. Run your three sensitivities, one at a time, and write down which lever crosses your ratio target first and which team owns it.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Why does payback divide CAC by monthly gross profit rather than monthly revenue?

A Cadence account pays $120 a month, but hosting, transcription and support take $30 of it at a 75% margin. Only $90 can pay back the $1,800, so payback is 20 months, not the 15 a revenue division would show.

2 · What did David Skok actually say about 12 months in "SaaS Metrics 2.0"?

Twelve months is his ceiling, the point past which he calls profitability anemic, not a target. The target, in his words, is the 5-7 months many of the best SaaS businesses manage. Quoting "Skok says under 12 months" turns a warning line into a goal.

3 · On a product that is one year old, which LTV assumption is most likely to break first?

At 2.5% a month the formula promises a 40-month average life. A one-year-old product has never watched a customer for 40 months; it has watched its first customers for at most twelve, and early churn is rarely the churn of later years. LTV does not include CAC, and practitioners do not agree on discounting.
