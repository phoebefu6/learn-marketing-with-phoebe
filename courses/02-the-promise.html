Part 1 · covers competitive alternatives

Start from what they would do instead

5 min live

Dunford's definition, from her own site: "Positioning defines how our product is different and better than alternatives for a particular set of customers." The word doing the work is alternatives. So her starting question is not a list of features but "What would a customer do if your offering didn't exist?" The answer is rarely the company on your competitor slide. It is usually a habit, a spreadsheet, a person, or nothing at all.

LiveThe four real alternatives for Cadence's beachhead4 min▶

Ask a research lead with a team of eight what happens to an interview today, and you get four answers (constructed for Cadence, but every research team will recognise them):

  1. A teammate types a shared doc live. It works, it costs a second person's hour per interview, and the quotes come out paraphrased.
  2. Record and rewatch. Accurate, and slow: about two more hours per interview before anything is written down.
  3. A general meeting-bot note-taker. A transcript per call, which is not the same as a finding. Nobody connects call 3 to call 17.
  4. Doing nothing. Memory, a few notes, a debrief on Friday. No extra hours, and most of the evidence gone by the time product asks.

Notice that only one of the four is a product you could put on a competitor slide. The other three are behaviour. That is normal. Doing nothing and the spreadsheet are usually the real competitor, because they cost nothing new, need no approval and carry no risk of a failed rollout. A promise that does not beat "carry on as we are" loses to it by default, however it compares with the other vendors.

Self-studyWhy the competitor list in a pitch deck is usually the wrong list3 min▶

A pitch-deck competitor slide lists the companies that look like you: same category word, similar homepage, often a two-by-two where you sit alone in the top-right corner. It is built for investors, who ask "who else is doing this". Buyers ask a different question: "what happens if I do not buy anything". Their shortlist is the status quo plus whatever they tried last time.

The practical fix is cheap. Ask five recent customers what they were doing the week before they bought, and ask five lost deals what they did instead. Write down the answers in their words. If "nothing" or "kept the spreadsheet" appears more than once, that is your primary alternative, and the positioning work starts there rather than with the vendor list.

Real world

Most leads chose to carry on as they were. In the public Olist marketing funnel, 8,000 sellers filled in a request to be contacted about selling on the marketplace, and 842 of them closed. Whatever else the other leads did, the record shows no deal: the alternative most of them chose was to carry on without the marketplace. (The deal log starts in December 2017, so a few early leads may have closed unrecorded; in the cleaner 2018 cohort, 781 of 5,998 closed, 13.0 percent.) This is the general pattern for any offer: the strongest rival of a marketplace pitching sellers is the seller's own status quo, not another marketplace.

Part 2 · covers unique attributes, value, best-fit customers

From what only you have to why it matters

5 min live

Dunford breaks positioning into five components, in this order on her site: competitive alternatives, key unique attributes, value, target customers, market category. The labels shift slightly between her pages and her book Obviously Awesome (2019), whose own wording is reported here rather than quoted; the order is what matters. Each component is built from the one before it, so a weak list of alternatives produces weak value, however good the copywriting.

LiveAttributes are facts, value is what the fact enables5 min▶

A key unique attribute is something Cadence has or does that the alternatives do not. It should be checkable: a feature, a method, a piece of expertise, a delivery model. "Powerful" and "intuitive" are not attributes; nobody can check them, and every rival claims them.

Value is what the attribute lets this customer do, stated against the alternative they would otherwise use. The attribute (constructed): Cadence links every quote to a research theme across all interviews. The value, against record-and-rewatch: a synthesis across twenty interviews in an afternoon instead of most of a week. Same fact, now with a unit the research lead already measures, her team's week.

Then target customers: not everyone who could use it, but the accounts who care most about that value. A solo freelancer with two interviews a month barely notices the saving; a team of eight running 5+ interviews a week notices it every Friday. That is why the build-along re-reads the session 1 ICP instead of writing a new one.

Self-studyThe quarter-inch hole, and whose line it is3 min▶

The most quoted version of the attribute-to-value idea is the drill and the "quarter-inch hole": people do not want the tool, they want the outcome it produces. It is a good idea and a badly attributed one. Slides usually credit it to Theodore Levitt.

Quote Investigator traced it. Its account, read at source: the earliest strong match is a 1942 insurance advertisement, it credits Leo McGivena, a newspaper publicity manager, and suspects the saying circulated even earlier; Levitt himself credited McGivena in his 1969 book The Marketing Mode, and a 2005 Harvard Business Review article by Clayton Christensen helped spread the attribution to Levitt. So the honest citation is "an idea usually credited to Levitt, who credited Leo McGivena".

The lesson for positioning is the same as the line's: buyers pay for the outcome against their current way of getting it. The lesson for your slides is separate: check the name before you put it under a quote.

Real world

A marketing truth with the wrong name on it. The drill line is routinely printed under Levitt's name, while, on Quote Investigator's account, Levitt pointed to someone else. It took a dedicated quote-tracing site to put McGivena's name back. If one of the most repeated sentences in marketing carries the wrong author, any quote on your own deck is worth a two-minute check against the earliest source you can find, and a quote you cannot trace is better paraphrased than misattributed.

Part 3 · covers the market category

The frame that makes the value obvious

5 min live

The last component is the one buyers notice first. The category is the short answer to "what is it", and buyers use it to decide what to compare you with before they read a word of your value. Pick a frame where your strongest attribute is the thing everyone is compared on, and the value looks obvious. Pick the crowded frame and the same product becomes one more option on a price list.

LiveOne product, two frames4 min▶

Put Cadence in the frame "meeting note-taker" and the buyer knows exactly what to do: line it up against the many note-taking bots, compare transcription accuracy, compare price per seat. That frame is crowded, priced like a utility, and graded on a test where Cadence is merely fine. Put the same product in "research repository for interview teams" and the shortlist changes to a shared doc, a research wiki and a spreadsheet, and the test becomes synthesis across interviews, the attribute Cadence is strongest on. Both comparisons are constructed for teaching; the mechanism is not.

The category decides which competitors the buyer lines you up against, and the competitors decide the criterion. Choose the frame by asking: in which frame is our strongest attribute the main thing people compare? A frame that is too new needs explaining before it helps; a frame that is too crowded hides you. The useful frame is one the buyer already understands, where you win on the test it implies.

Self-studyTrends as a layer, and where the positioning statement lives3 min▶

Book summaries of Obviously Awesome report that Dunford treats a relevant trend as an optional layer added on top of the five components, not a sixth component, and that she warns against bolting a fashionable trend onto a product it does not fit. That is reported from summaries, not quoted. The working rule follows from the five-component logic anyway: a trend helps only if it makes your value more urgent for your best-fit customers, and it hurts if it puts you in the crowded frame.

Turning a finished positioning into a brand positioning statement ("For [audience] who [moment], Cadence is the [category] that...") and testing whether the category words sound like the buyer's own speech is brand work, taught in the AI branding course's positioning page: learn-ai-branding b2, positioning strategy. Tonight is the decision underneath that statement. Bring tonight's canvas to that page and the statement nearly writes itself.

Real world

"Better" only exists relative to something. Dunford's own definition carries the whole point of this part: "Positioning defines how our product is different and better than alternatives for a particular set of customers." Better than which alternatives, for which customers, is exactly what the category frame sets. Apply it to any note-taker on the market: in the frame of meeting tools it is one of many, compared on transcription; in the frame of research repositories for interview teams it may be the only one built for synthesis. The product did not change. The alternatives did.

Build-along

Position Cadence on one canvas

★ 22 min · everyone builds

A sheet of paper folded into six boxes, or six rows in a spreadsheet. Bring the session 1 ICP card. You will fill the five components in Dunford's order and then run one test that edits the result. Every entry below is constructed for Cadence; when you run it on your own product, the alternatives must come from real customers' words.

List the four alternatives and what each costs. Write them as behaviour, in the buyer's words, with a cost in a unit she already tracks: hours, people, lost evidence. At least one should be "do nothing".

★ Step 1 · alternatives and costs (constructed)1. A teammate types a shared doc live cost: a second person's hour per interview; quotes paraphrased 2. Record and rewatch cost: about 2 more hours per interview before anything is written 3. A general meeting-bot note-taker cost: a transcript per call; nobody connects call 3 to call 17 4. Do nothing: memory, a few notes, a Friday debrief cost: no extra hours; most quotes gone when product asks

List three attributes Cadence has that none of the alternatives has. Facts only: something a sceptical buyer could check in a demo. If a line contains "easy", "powerful" or "seamless", it is not an attribute yet.

★ Step 2 · attributes, facts only (constructed)A. Links every quote to a research theme across all interviews B. Every quote opens the exact clip it came from C. Product managers can search the repository themselves

Turn each attribute into value for the research lead, against a named alternative. The pattern is: what she can now do, compared with what she does now. Name the alternative in brackets so the test in step 6 is easy.

★ Step 3 · value, each against an alternativeA. A synthesis across 20 interviews in an afternoon, instead of most of a week (vs record and rewatch) B. Quotes product trusts, because each one opens its clip (vs the paraphrased live doc) C. Product answers its own questions from the repository, instead of asking the research team again (vs doing nothing)

Who cares most: re-read the session 1 ICP and mark which value lines matter most to it. Do not write a new audience; rank the value for the one you already chose.

★ Step 4 · who cares most (from the session 1 ICP)ICP: research lead, team of 5 to 15 at a software firm, 5+ recorded interviews a week; trigger: a new head of research, or a new shared research repository Cares most: A (her team's week back) and C (fewer repeat requests) Cares less: B, until a finding is challenged in a product review

Choose the category frame and name the three competitors it implies. Write both frames you considered, the competitors each implies, and the test the buyer will use in each. Pick the one where your strongest value line is the test.

★ Step 5 · the frame and its rivals (constructed)Frame A: meeting note-taker rivals: many note-taking bots test: transcription, price per seat Frame B: research repository for interview teams rivals: a shared doc, a research wiki, a spreadsheet test: synthesis across interviews CHOSEN: matches value A

Run the alternative test. Read each value line next to each alternative from step 1. A value line that does not beat at least one named alternative is cut, however nice it sounds. This is the step that keeps the website honest.

★ Step 6 · the alternative testA. beats record and rewatch, and doing nothing keep B. beats the paraphrased live doc keep C. beats doing nothing keep D. "Easy to use": the live doc is easy too, and doing nothing is easiest of all CUT

Keep the canvas. Session 3 picks channels by asking where these research leads already gather, and the value lines you kept tonight become the first message each channel test carries. If the alternatives in box 1 change after real customer interviews, redo boxes 3 to 6: everything downstream was built on them.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Why does Dunford's method start from competitive alternatives rather than from the feature list?

Her starting question is "What would a customer do if your offering didn't exist?" A feature is unique only against something, and valuable only compared with the buyer's current way. Start from the features and you describe the product; start from the alternatives and you describe why it is better.

2 · Which pair is a correct attribute and value for Cadence?

An attribute is a checkable fact; value is what the fact lets this customer do, against a named alternative. Option B has them the wrong way round, and A and C contain no fact anyone could check in a demo.

3 · A slide puts the "quarter-inch hole" line under Theodore Levitt's name. What does Quote Investigator's trace say?

Levitt's 1969 book The Marketing Mode credits McGivena, and a 2005 Harvard Business Review article by Christensen helped spread the attribution to Levitt. An uncertain origin is a reason to drop the name, never a licence to pick one.
