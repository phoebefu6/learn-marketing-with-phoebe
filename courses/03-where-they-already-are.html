Where they already are

You know who Cadence is for and what it promises them. The next question is where to say it, and the honest answer is that most teams never really ask it: they reach for the two channels they already know and call that a strategy. Tonight we lay out every door, test three of them for less than a thousand dollars each, write the kill line before the money moves, and retire two famous marketing numbers that nobody ever measured.

Part 0

Where we start

Session 1 gave Cadence a beachhead: user-research teams of 5 to 15 at software firms, bought by a research lead, triggered by a new head of research or a new shared research repository. Session 2 gave it a promise against the real alternative. Tonight is about reach: which channels put that promise in front of those research leads, and how to find out cheaply before committing a quarter's budget. Part 1 · covers the nineteen traction channels

Nineteen doors, and most teams try two

5 min live

Gabriel Weinberg wrote up a simple discipline with Justin Mares in Traction (2014, revised 2015) and in two posts of his own: "The Bullseye Framework for Getting Traction" and "The 19 channels you can use to get traction". The discipline is three rings. Brainstorm every channel, test the few that look probable, focus on the one that works. The point of the outer ring is not the list; it is refusing to skip the channels you have never tried.

LiveWhy you brainstorm all nineteen, even the silly ones4 min▶

Teams default to the channels they already know. A founder from a sales background reaches for outbound; a marketer from a content team reaches for a blog and SEO. Neither is wrong, but neither was chosen. The outer ring, which Weinberg calls "The Outer Ring: What's Possible", exists to force one concrete idea for every channel before anything is ruled out. The best channel is often the one nobody in the room has used, precisely because competitors have not used it either.

His nineteen channels, in his order and with his headings:

  1. Targeting Blogs
  2. Publicity
  3. Unconventional PR
  4. Search Engine Marketing
  5. Social and Display Ads
  6. Offline Ads
  7. Search Engine Optimization
  8. Content Marketing
  9. Email Marketing
  10. Viral Marketing
  11. Engineering as Marketing
  12. Business Development
  13. Sales
  14. Affiliate Programs
  15. Existing Platforms
  16. Trade Shows
  17. Offline Events
  18. Speaking Engagements
  19. Community Building

Then the rings move inward: "The Middle Ring: What's Probable" holds the few you will test, and "The Inner Ring: What's Working" holds the one that earned the focus. A channel can always move back out when it stops working.

Self-studyThe fit question: where does the session 1 ICP already gather?3 min▶

A channel is only as good as its overlap with the people you chose. The ICP from session 1 is a research lead at a software firm with 5 to 15 researchers, five or more recorded interviews a week. So the useful question is not "which channel is hot" but "where do these people already spend attention, unprompted?"

For research leads that points at a short, specific list: practitioner research communities and Slack groups, a handful of research newsletters and blogs they read every week, and a few research conferences where they give and attend talks. Each one maps onto a Weinberg heading: Community Building, Targeting Blogs, Speaking Engagements. Search Engine Marketing fits less well, because few people search for a category they do not yet know exists.

Remember the trigger as well. A new head of research is reachable in a different place from a team that is starting a shared repository; the first reads leadership posts, the second asks peers for tool recommendations. One ICP can point at two different channels depending on which trigger you are chasing.

Real world

Olist's leads came through a few doors, and one door nobody can name. In the real Olist funnel, the 5,998 leads of the 2018 cohort arrived through organic search 1,736, paid search 1,182, social 1,051, unknown 813, direct traffic 380, email 347, referral 211 and display 75, with the small remainder spread across "other" sources or left blank. The top three channels brought about two thirds of all leads (3,969 of 5,998). That is the normal shape: a mix concentrated in a few channels, plus 813 leads whose origin nobody recorded. The concentration is a choice the team made, knowingly or not; the 813 are a measurement gap, and Part 3 comes back to them.

Part 2 · covers cheap tests

The middle ring is a set of cheap tests

5 min live

The middle ring is not a commitment; it is a shopping list of experiments. Weinberg's instruction is direct: "For each traction channel in your middle ring, now construct a cheap traction test you can run to determine if the idea really is good or not." His size for cheap is equally direct: enough to get "a rough idea of a channel's effectiveness with at most a thousand dollars and a month of time". A test that costs a quarter's budget is not a test, it is a bet.

LiveThe test card: five lines, written before the money moves4 min▶

Every middle-ring test fits on an index card, and the card is written before the test starts:

  1. Channel and idea. The Weinberg heading and the specific move: not "blogs" but "sponsor two named research newsletters for one issue each".
  2. The one metric. Cost per qualified lead, where qualified means the lead matches the session 1 ICP. Ideally cost per closed customer too, but a month is usually too short for deals, so state which one you will judge on.
  3. The budget cap. A number, under $1,000, and it is a ceiling, not a target.
  4. The date. The day you read the result, about a month out.
  5. The kill line. The result that ends the test, plus the minimum sample you need before a pass counts. Cadence uses above $150 per qualified lead, and 10 or more leads to pass.

The order matters. A kill line written after the results arrive is not a threshold; it is a story told about the results.

Self-studyA test that cannot fail is not a test3 min▶

If no possible result would make you stop, the money bought reassurance, not information. Three common ways a test is rigged without anyone meaning to: the metric is chosen after the data arrives (clicks looked bad, so the team reports impressions); the budget has no cap, so a slow channel simply gets more money until it "works"; or the sample is too small to say anything and gets read as a win anyway.

That last one is why the figure has three outcomes, not two. Speaking at one conference produced four qualified leads at $100 each, under the kill line but far below the 10 leads a pass needs. Inconclusive is an honest verdict. It means either run it again with a bigger sample or park it; it never means promote it.

Time matters as much as money. Traction is reported as recommending that a startup spend about half its time on getting traction rather than only on the product; treat that as the authors' rule of thumb, reported here from summaries, not as a measured finding. For the statistics of test design, sample sizes and stopping rules, see Learn Experimentation with Phoebe ↗.

Real world

Small channels need a sample before they need a verdict. In the real Olist 2018 cohort, display brought 75 leads and 5 deals, a 6.7 percent close rate whose 95 percent range runs from 2.9 to 14.7 percent. Referral brought 211 leads and 24 deals, 11.4 percent, range 7.8 to 16.4. Both look like a number, but the range on display is wide enough to hold a poor channel and a good one. That is the inconclusive card in real data: before you kill a small channel or crown it, check how many outcomes the rate stands on. Session 4 puts every one of these ranges on the bench.

Part 3 · covers folklore numbers

Numbers everyone repeats and nobody measured

5 min live

Channel meetings run on a few famous numbers. Two of them come up constantly: a prospect needs seven touches before buying, and half of all advertising money is wasted. Both sound like research. When you trace them back, neither leads to a study; one leads to old trade items repeated by a blog, the other to a saying with three possible owners. A professional can still use the idea behind each, but should never present either as a finding.

LiveThe "rule of 7": folklore with a long tail4 min▶

The rule says a prospect has to see or hear from you seven times before they act. It is repeated in sales training, agency decks and channel plans, and there is no primary study behind it. The trail that does exist is reported, not read: a 2010 blog post traced the idea to an 1859 item about the sixth sighting of an advertisement, and to Thomas Smith's 1885 list of twenty exposures. Neither is a measurement; both are advertising wisdom written as a list. And the number in the list is not even seven.

Teach it as folklore and keep the useful kernel: most people do not buy on first contact, so plan more than one touch. How many touches your buyers need is an empirical question about your own funnel, answered in your own data, not a constant you can import.

Self-study"Half the money I spend on advertising is wasted", and which half3 min▶

The line is usually given to the American retailer John Wanamaker. Quote Investigator, whose account was read at source, finds no firm evidence that he said it. The earliest Wanamaker-linked evidence it reports is from 1919, and second-hand; a version linked to the British soap maker Lord Leverhulme appears in 1931, also second-hand, through his son; and David Ogilvy in 1963 credited both men. So the honest form is "attributed to Wanamaker", or better, "an old saying".

The saying survives because the question behind it is real: which half? That question is attribution, deciding how much credit each channel deserves for a customer who touched several. It is a discipline of its own, with its own models and its own disagreements, and it lives in Learn Marketing Attribution with Phoebe ↗. Here, the point is narrower: you cannot find the wasted half of a channel you cannot see.

Real world

The honest version of "which half" is a column called unknown. In the real Olist 2018 cohort, 813 leads have the origin "unknown", and they closed at 20.7 percent (168 deals), against 13.0 percent for the cohort as a whole. The best-converting large group of leads is the one nobody can attribute to a channel. Untracked does not mean bad; it means invisible, and you cannot spend more on a channel you cannot see. Session 4 starts from exactly this: what a budget split looks like when one of its best sources has no name.

Homework

Before session 4

30 min
  1. Run the outer ring on your own product. All nineteen channels, one specific idea each. Circle the ones your team has never tried.
  2. Score a shortlist of six against your ICP on where they already are, cost to test and speed to signal. Write one line of evidence beside every 3.
  3. Write three test cards with a metric, a cap under $1,000 and a month, a kill line and a minimum sample, and show them to one colleague before any money is spent.
  4. Find your own "unknown". Pull last quarter's leads and count how many have no recorded source. Then compute their close rate against the rest, the same comparison as the Olist 813.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · What is the middle ring of the Bullseye for?

Weinberg calls it "The Middle Ring: What's Probable", and asks for a cheap traction test for each channel in it. It is a set of experiments, not commitments; what is already working belongs to the inner ring.

2 · Why is the kill threshold written before the test starts?

A test that cannot fail is not a test. Writing the kill line, the metric and the minimum sample first is what turns a month of spend into a decision you can trust.

3 · A colleague says "prospects need seven touches before they buy, that is the research". What is the accurate reply?

The kernel, that most buyers need more than one touch, is sensible. The number is not a finding. The 1885 list of twenty exposures is part of the same folklore, reported second-hand, not a better study.
