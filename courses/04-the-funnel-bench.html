The funnel bench

Session 3 chose channels by asking where the customers already are. Tonight the question gets a price. A Brazilian marketplace published its real sales funnel: every lead that asked to sell on it, the channel each lead came from, and which ones became paying sellers. Those rows sit in this page. You split a month of budget across six channels, the page counts what the split buys in leads, deals, cost per customer and months to earn it back, and one button breaks the data on purpose to prove the bench measures what it says it does.

Part 0

Where we start

The data is the public Marketing Funnel by Olist: 8,000 marketing-qualified leads, sellers who filled in a request to sell on the Olist Store between June 2017 and June 2018, and the 842 of them that closed. Each lead carries the channel it arrived through. That is everything a channel decision needs on the conversion side, and nothing on the cost side, because the dataset has no spend. So the bench splits the job honestly: what happened is measured from the rows; what things cost is an assumption panel you can see and change.

Part 1 · covers leads, close rate and CAC

A lead is a cost, a deal is a customer

9 min live

Money buys leads at some cost per lead. Leads become customers at some close rate. Customer acquisition cost, CAC, is the money divided by the customers, and nothing else. Two multipliers sit between the budget and the CAC, and a channel can win the first and lose the second.

LiveTwo multipliers, one division4 min▶

Cost per lead turns money into leads. It is never flat: the first leads from a channel come from the people most ready to respond, and each extra dollar reaches people slightly less ready. The bench models that with a curve that starts at the stated cost per lead and flattens toward a cap, set by default at twice the channel's real monthly lead volume. That curve is constructed and labelled; the shape is the standard one, the numbers are not anybody's measurements.

Close rate turns leads into customers. It is measured, per channel, from which of the 5,998 leads in the 2018 cohort became sellers. It varies by more than three to one across the channels: paid search 14.8 percent, social 6.3 percent, email 4.0 percent.

CAC is the budget divided by the deals. A channel with cheap leads and a poor close rate can have a worse CAC than one with expensive leads that close. Social's assumed cost per lead is $25 against paid search's $60; at the first dollar, social's CAC is about $25 / 0.063, roughly $400, and paid search's is about $60 / 0.148, roughly $405. Nearly a tie, from very different routes.

LiveWhy the bench starts in January 20183 min▶

The closed-deal records begin on 5 December 2017. Leads that arrived in 2017 had their deals logged, if at all, only from that date on, so the 2,002 leads from 2017 show just 61 closes, 3.0 percent, against 13.0 percent for the 5,998 leads from January to May 2018. The 2017 leads did not get worse. The deal log started late.

The bench defaults to the 2018 cohort for that reason, and gives you a checkbox to include 2017 anyway. Tick it and every CAC on the ladder rises, the even split from $524 to $742, because a third of the leads now look as if they never close. The ordering of the presets survives. The dollar figures do not, which is the general rule for any funnel number taken from a log whose start date you have not checked.

Real world

The channel nobody can buy converts best. 813 of the 2018 leads have origin "unknown", and they close at 20.7 percent, the highest rate of any large group in the table. Untracked does not mean bad; it often means the lead came through a route the tracking never saw, a conversation, a forwarded link, a salesperson's own contact. The bench cannot spend on "unknown", and neither can you. What you can do is fix the tracking, which is the subject of the attribution course rather than this one.

Build-along · the bench

Six channels, one budget, computed in front of you

★ 26 min · everyone builds

Every count in the table is computed from the real rows when the page loads. Every dollar comes from the assumptions panel. Work down the steps below the bench in order; each one prints a number this page quotes, and if your bench prints something different, check whether you changed an assumption.

Read the real table before touching a dollar. Leave every assumption at its default. Find the two channels that bring the most leads, and the two that close best. On this data they are the same two, organic and paid search, which matters in step 3.

Part 2 · covers the anti-lever

Most leads, fewer customers

5 min live

"Put the money where the leads come from" is the most natural sentence in a budget meeting, and on this data it is wrong, though by less than you might hope. It buys 488.8 leads, more than any other preset, and 50.5 customers, fewer than weighting by deals. The gap is $16 a customer, 4.3 percent. Small, because the two biggest lead sources here are also the two best closers. All of the damage comes from one channel.

LiveWhy the gap is small here, and when it is not3 min▶

Weighting by leads and weighting by deals give the same answer wherever a channel's share of leads equals its share of deals. Organic search is 28.9 percent of the 2018 leads and 32.7 percent of the deals; paid search 19.7 and 22.4. Close. Social is 17.5 and 8.5. Not close. The lead-weighted split sends social roughly twice the money its deals justify, and that is the whole $16.

So the honest lesson is not "volume is always a trap". It is that volume is the right signal only to the extent that conversion is uniform, and you cannot know whether it is uniform without looking. On a funnel where the biggest lead source is also the worst closer, the same sentence would cost far more than 4.3 percent. Check the second column before the first one wins the meeting.

Self-studyThe bigger trap: cheapest leads first3 min▶

Cost per lead is the metric ad platforms report first, and the cheapest-leads preset follows it: email at an assumed $15 and social at $25 get the most money. They are the two worst closers in the table, so the split buys 412.1 leads and only 31.9 customers, CAC $627, 70.4 percent above the best split. A lower cost per lead bought a higher cost per customer.

The fix is the same in both traps: rank channels by what a customer costs, never by what a lead costs, and treat cost per lead as an input to CAC rather than a result.

Real world

Social, in this funnel, is a lead machine and a weak closer. 1,051 real 2018 leads, the third biggest source, and 66 deals. Whether those leads were poorly targeted, arrived earlier in their decision, or were handled differently by sales, the rows cannot say. What they can say is that a budget meeting looking only at the lead column would have scaled the channel that converts at under half the rate of the two above it.

Part 3 · covers small samples, missing revenue and the break button

What is measured, what is assumed, what is too thin to trust

5 min live

Every close rate on the bench is a fact about real rows, and some of those facts rest on a handful of deals. Display's 6.7 percent is five deals out of 75 leads; the 95 percent range around it runs from 2.9 to 14.7 percent, wide enough to contain both the worst and the best channel in the table. A bench that printed 6.7 without the range would be telling you something it does not know.

LiveThe revenue column that is mostly zeros3 min▶

The dataset has a field called declared monthly revenue on each closed deal. It would be the obvious source for payback. It cannot be used: 797 of the 842 closed deals declare 0, and the 45 that declare something range from 6 to 50,000,000 reais. The median of all closed deals is zero. The table on the bench shows the counts per channel, "12 of 255 declared" for organic, so you can see why.

So payback uses one stated assumption for every channel: $80 a month of revenue per new seller at a 65 percent gross margin, $52 a month of gross profit, and payback is CAC divided by 52. Because the assumption is the same everywhere, payback ranks the presets exactly as CAC does. Session 5 is where revenue per customer becomes the subject rather than a constant.

Self-studyWhy a break button, and how it works3 min▶

A bench that can only ever report the answer you hoped for measures nothing. The break button takes the 781 closed flags and deals them back out across the 5,998 leads with a fixed random seed, so the number of deals stays 781 but which channel they land in is now arbitrary. If the bench's ranking came from the channels, the ranking should collapse. It does: lead-weighted and deal-weighted tie at $316, because with conversion uniform, volume is the right signal after all.

It also moves the "best split" toward the cheap-lead channels, social and email, which is what you would expect once every channel converts alike: then cost per lead is the only difference left. That is the mirror image of step 6, and the reason step 6 loses on the real data.

Real world

The 30-deal flag is a convention, not a law. The bench marks any channel with fewer than 30 closed deals and prints the 95 percent range beside every rate. There is nothing magic about 30; what matters is that the range is printed. Referral's 11.4 percent could be anywhere from 7.8 to 16.4. Before moving a budget on it, run the cheap test session 3 described and collect more deals.

Homework

Before session 5

30 min
  1. Pull your own lead table: leads and closed deals by channel for one cohort whose deals have had time to close. Check when your deal log starts before you trust any rate.
  2. Compute each channel's share of leads and share of deals. Circle the channel where the two differ most. That channel is where "most leads" costs you money.
  3. Print the 95 percent range for every rate built on fewer than 30 deals, and write "too thin to move budget on" beside it.
  4. Write down your real cost per lead for two channels, or your best guess with its source. Session 5 turns CAC into payback and lifetime value, and it needs your numbers, not the bench's.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · The "most leads" preset buys 488.8 leads and the deals-weighted preset 436.9. Why does the second still have the lower CAC?

Both presets spend the same $20,000. CAC is budget over deals, so the preset with more deals wins. Leads are an input; a split that buys more of them from a weak closer can end with fewer customers.

2 · After pressing "Break it", most leads and deals-weighted both print $316. What does that prove?

A break button is a test of the instrument. If shuffling the very thing a preset relies on removes its advantage, the advantage was coming from that thing and not from an artifact of the bench.

3 · Display closes at 6.7 percent on 5 deals, range 2.9 to 14.7 percent. What is the right budget decision?

Five deals cannot rank a channel. The range is the honest statement of what the data knows, and it spans most of the table. The decision it supports is to buy information cheaply, not to move a budget either way.
